Price Tag & Pick Lab

Price the menu. Hire the barista. Survive the 9-to-5. Watch where your brain helps you and where it fools you.

Theory behind this
  • Anchoring: the first or highest number you see colours how you judge the rest. Tversky & Kahneman (1974), Judgment under uncertainty, Science.
  • Decoy effect: adding a clearly worse option makes a nearby option look better. Huber, Payne & Puto (1982), Journal of Consumer Research.
  • Loss aversion: losing something hurts more than gaining the same thing feels good. Kahneman & Tversky (1979), Prospect theory, Econometrica.
  • Halo effect: one standout trait bleeds into ratings of unrelated traits. Thorndike (1920), A constant error in psychological ratings, Journal of Applied Psychology.
  • Structured selection: consistent, job-related scoring tends to predict performance better than unstructured impressions. Schmidt & Hunter (1998), Psychological Bulletin.

Authors and years are from memory. Verify titles, journals and page details before citing.

Method and limits
  • Simulated, not real data. The 100 shoppers are generated with a fixed random seed. Each has made-up values for how much they like coffee and how much price bothers them.
  • Anchoring, decoy and loss-framing boosts are illustrative numbers I chose, not effect sizes from research. The page shows direction, not magnitude.
  • The six candidates are invented. Their charm and skill scores were written so charm and skill don’t line up. That makes the halo trap visible by design, so it demonstrates the idea rather than proving it.
  • No participant data is collected or stored. Everything runs in your browser.
  • For a write-up: treat this as a teaching tool and a prototype for a real experiment (for example, randomising menus across real participants).
